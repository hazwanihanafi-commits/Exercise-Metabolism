02 • CASE 01 — FASTING

FASTING

Why can fasting change breath odour?

With prolonged fasting, carbohydrate availability falls and fat mobilisation increases.

Mechanism clue: fatty acids → β-oxidation → acetyl-CoA → liver ketogenesis → ketone bodies.

Your question: What changed in substrate availability?

Model: With prolonged fasting, liver glycogen becomes limited and fatty-acid oxidation increases. The liver produces ketone bodies; acetone is volatile and can contribute to fruity/acetone-like breath odour.

03 • CASE 02 — CARBOHYDRATE LOADING

CARBOHYDRATE LOADING

Why carbohydrate-load before a marathon?

Follow carbohydrate from food to stored fuel.

Mechanism: carbohydrate → glucose → muscle/liver glycogen → greater carbohydrate availability during prolonged exercise.

Question: Does carbohydrate loading create ATP directly?

No. It increases stored carbohydrate availability before prolonged exercise. During exercise, glycogen supplies glucose for glycolysis and subsequent oxidative ATP production.

04 • CASE 03 — SPRINT

SPRINT

Why does the first few seconds feel explosive?

ATP demand rises almost immediately, before oxidative metabolism can fully increase.

Mechanism: stored ATP + phosphocreatine → rapid ATP resynthesis.

Question: Which pathway has the fastest ATP regeneration rate?

ATP-PCr. The ATP-PCr system can regenerate ATP very rapidly and contributes strongly during very short, high-power efforts. It does not work alone; glycolytic and oxidative pathways also contribute as exercise continues.

05 • CASE 04 — 400 m + LACTATE

400 m

Why does lactate rise?

ATP demand is very high and glycolytic flux increases substantially.

Mechanism: glycogen/glucose → glycolysis → pyruvate → lactate formation when lactate appearance exceeds immediate utilisation/clearance.

Question: Is lactate simply “waste”?

No. Lactate is a metabolically useful intermediate that can be transported and oxidised or used in other pathways. Blood lactate reflects the balance between appearance and removal, not simply a fatigue toxin.

06 • CASE 05 — 5 km + VO₂

5 KM

Why does VO₂ matter?

A sustained running effort requires substantial aerobic ATP production.

Mechanism: O₂ consumption reflects the rate of aerobic oxidative metabolism under appropriate conditions.

Question: What other variable helps explain the metabolic picture?

Interpret VO₂ alongside workload, HR, VCO₂, RER, ventilation and RPE. Together these variables help explain the relationship between external demand and internal physiological response.

07 • CASE 06 — THE “WALL”

THE “WALL”

Why might a marathoner suddenly slow down?

The athlete still has stored fat, but ATP supply rate may become limiting at the required intensity.

Mechanism: glycogen availability ↓ → greater relative reliance on fat oxidation → ATP supply rate may not support the same high exercise intensity.

Question: Is “having energy” the same as “producing ATP fast enough”?

No. This is the distinction between energy capacity and rate of ATP production. Fat stores have high energy capacity, while carbohydrate pathways can support a higher rate of ATP supply during intense exercise.

08 • CASE 07 — RECOVERY

RECOVERY

Why is VO₂ still elevated after exercise?

Exercise has stopped, but recovery processes continue.

Mechanism: PCr restoration + thermoregulation + cardiovascular recovery + metabolic processes contribute to elevated post-exercise oxygen consumption.

Question: What could you monitor?

Monitor HR recovery, VO₂ recovery when available, RPE and other relevant physiological variables. The pattern provides information about how the body returns toward baseline.

09 • CASE 08 — SAME SPEED, DIFFERENT RESPONSE

SAME SPEED, DIFFERENT RESPONSE

Why can two people have different HR and RPE?

The external demand can be similar while the internal physiological cost differs.

Mechanism clue: fitness, cardiovascular function, metabolic efficiency, training status, fatigue and health status can alter internal response.

Question: What is the key distinction?

External workload ≠ internal physiological load. This becomes important when interpreting exercise assessment and later when prescribing and monitoring exercise.

Interpretation rule: RER = VCO₂/VO₂ is most straightforward for estimating relative carbohydrate and fat oxidation during appropriate steady-state exercise. At high intensities, additional CO₂ from bicarbonate buffering can elevate RER, so it should not be interpreted as a simple “fuel percentage.”

11 • GRAND DETECTIVE CHALLENGE

An athlete increases treadmill speed. HR, VO₂, VCO₂ and RPE all rise. What changed first?
Now write the full detective chain in one sentence.
Model: Start with the exercise demand, identify the available fuel and relative pathway contribution, consider the required ATP rate, choose measurements that provide evidence, interpret them in context, then consider recovery.

THE DETECTIVE'S RULE:

Begin with WHAT IS THE DEMAND?

Then ask:
