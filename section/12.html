Fat & Protein Metabolism

Not all exercise fuel arrives through carbohydrate. Follow how fatty acids are oxidised through β-oxidation and how amino acids can contribute through their carbon skeletons while nitrogen is handled separately.

PART 01

WHY DO WE NEED MORE THAN ONE FUEL?

Concept

Exercise metabolism is not “carbohydrate OR fat”. Multiple substrates can be available at the same time. Their relative contribution changes with exercise intensity, duration, training status and substrate availability.

Carbohydrate

Useful for rapid ATP provision and especially relevant as exercise intensity rises.

Fat

Large energy capacity, but a slower rate of ATP supply than carbohydrate.

Protein

Primary role is structural and functional; usually a smaller exercise-fuel contribution.

Think before you learn: A long-duration athlete can rely on fat for substantial energy without fat becoming the only fuel.

PART 02

FAT → β-OXIDATION → ACETYL-CoA

Mechanism

Fatty acids can enter mitochondria and undergo repeated cycles of β-oxidation. Each cycle shortens the fatty acid and produces acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH₂.

What enters?

Fatty acids enter mitochondrial oxidative metabolism after reaching the mitochondria.

What is produced?

Repeated β-oxidation produces acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH₂.

Why does it matter?

These products feed the oxidative machinery that supports ATP production.

High-yield fact: Fat is an important high-capacity fuel, but its rate of ATP provision is slower than carbohydrate.
PART 03

RATE ≠ CAPACITY

Compare

A pathway can contribute a large amount of energy over time without delivering ATP at the fastest possible rate. This is why fat can support prolonged exercise while carbohydrate remains important when ATP must be supplied rapidly.

PART 04

PROTEIN → AMINO ACIDS → CARBON SKELETON + NITROGEN

Mechanism

Protein is broken down into amino acids. During energy metabolism, amino acids may be deaminated. The resulting carbon skeleton can enter pathways linked to energy production, while nitrogen must be handled separately.

Carbon skeleton

Can enter metabolic pathways linked to TCA intermediates or other energy-related routes.

Nitrogen

Must be handled separately and ultimately excreted through nitrogen-disposal pathways.

Exercise context: Protein usually contributes less to exercise energy than carbohydrate and fat, but its contribution may rise during prolonged exercise or when glycogen availability is low.

PART 05

HOW EXERCISE CHANGES SUBSTRATE USE

Apply

As exercise intensity and duration change, the relative contribution of different fuels changes too. Endurance training can also affect the body's ability to use fat during exercise.

Choose the exercise contextModerate continuous

Moderate continuous

Mixed fuel use can be substantial; fat oxidation may make an important contribution.

What to ask?

What is the intensity? How long is the exercise? What substrates are available?

Training effect

Endurance training can increase the relative capacity to oxidise fat during exercise.

PART 06

APPLY IT — THE ENDURANCE ATHLETE

Case reasoning

A runner is preparing for a long endurance event.

Use the chain: DEMAND → SUBSTRATE → PATHWAY → PRODUCTS → ATP SUPPLY. Explain why fat can make a substantial contribution during prolonged exercise, while carbohydrate remains important.

1 • DEMANDLong duration means ATP must be supplied continuously.
2 • SUBSTRATEFatty acids and carbohydrate-derived substrates are available.
3 • PATHWAYFatty acids can undergo β-oxidation; products feed oxidative ATP production.
Model answer: During prolonged exercise, fatty acids can enter mitochondria and undergo β-oxidation, producing acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH₂. These products support oxidative ATP production. Fat has a large energy capacity, so it can make a substantial contribution during prolonged exercise. Carbohydrate remains important because it can provide ATP more rapidly, especially when exercise intensity rises. The relative contribution of each substrate is influenced by intensity, duration, training status and substrate availability.
Common misconception: “Fat burns only when carbohydrate is absent.” Not supported. Carbohydrate and fat can be oxidised simultaneously; their relative contribution changes with the exercise context.
