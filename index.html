PART 1

Why Does Exercise Need Energy?

Every muscle contraction requires chemical energy. The immediate molecule used for cellular work is ATP.

ATP

Immediate energy currency for cellular work.

ATP stores

Limited. They cannot sustain prolonged exercise by themselves.

ATP regeneration

Must occur continuously as exercise continues.

ATP → ADP + Pi + energy for cellular work

Think Before Reveal

If food contains chemical energy, why can't the muscle simply use the carbohydrate or fat directly for every contraction?

Food nutrients must be processed through metabolic pathways. ATP provides the immediate transferable energy used for cellular work. Therefore, exercise metabolism is fundamentally about maintaining ATP supply to match ATP demand.

Key takeaway

Food is the source of nutrients and stored chemical energy; ATP is the immediate energy currency.

Case: carbohydrate-rich meal

A student eats a carbohydrate-rich meal two hours before exercise. What can happen to the carbohydrate?

Carbohydrate is digested and absorbed. Glucose may be used by tissues, stored as glycogen, or contribute to metabolic pathways supporting ATP regeneration. The size and timing of the meal, insulin response, glycogen status and subsequent exercise demand all influence what happens.

Phosphagen

Very rapid ATP regeneration. Important when ATP demand rises suddenly and greatly.

Glycolytic

Rapid ATP support through carbohydrate metabolism; important during higher-intensity work.

Oxidative

Slower but substantial ATP production capacity; supports sustained activity.

Exercise Intensity, Duration and Substrate Use

Never interpret substrate contribution using intensity alone. Consider intensity + duration + training status + nutritional state + environmental/individual factors.

SituationMetabolic thinking
Very short maximal effortRapid ATP regeneration is essential; phosphagen contribution is important.
High-intensity repeated intervalsRapid pathways contribute strongly, with oxidative contribution during work and recovery.
Moderate prolonged exerciseOxidative metabolism is important; carbohydrate and fat both contribute.
Very prolonged exerciseDuration, glycogen availability, fat mobilisation, nutrition and pacing become increasingly relevant.

Case A — 10-second maximal sprint

Which energy demand characteristic is most important?

ATP must be regenerated extremely rapidly. The phosphagen system is highly relevant, while other pathways also contribute depending on the exact duration and context.

Case B — 90-minute moderate cycling

Why would it be inappropriate to say “only the aerobic system is working”?

Energy pathways overlap. Oxidative metabolism is highly important during prolonged moderate exercise, but ATP regeneration remains an integrated process with contributions from multiple pathways.
PART 5

Fasting, Feeding and Carbohydrate Availability

Fasted exercise

After an overnight fast, nutritional conditions differ from the fed state. Hormonal regulation, liver glycogen availability, adipose tissue lipolysis and exercise intensity influence substrate availability and utilisation.

Morning fasted exercise

A person performs 45 minutes of moderate exercise before breakfast.

Predict: What changes in fuel availability might influence the response?

Fasting changes circulating nutrient availability and promotes mobilisation of stored fuels. Fat mobilisation and oxidation can become more prominent, while carbohydrate availability and utilisation remain dependent on glycogen stores, exercise intensity and individual factors.

Carbohydrate loading

For selected endurance events, carbohydrate availability is relevant because muscle glycogen is an important fuel reserve during prolonged exercise. Nutritional strategy should match the planned exercise demand.

Clinical Exercise Scientist Question

If two clients perform the same 60-minute exercise session but have different nutritional states, should you automatically assume their metabolic responses are identical?

No. Exercise metabolism is influenced by both the exercise stimulus and the physiological/nutritional context of the individual.
PART 6

Why Can Breath Smell Change?

This is a useful example of connecting a simple observation to metabolism.

Observation

A person has been fasting for a prolonged period and notices a distinctive breath odour.

What metabolic clue could you consider?

During prolonged fasting or low carbohydrate availability, increased fat metabolism can contribute to ketone body production in the liver. Acetone is volatile and can contribute to a characteristic breath odour. This is a metabolic clue, not by itself a diagnostic test.
Clinical reasoning: One observation rarely proves one mechanism. Use it as a clue and integrate it with history, context and appropriate physiological measurements.
PART 7

What Should We Monitor During Exercise?

To understand exercise demand and response, the exercise scientist needs observable variables.

Heart rate

Useful indicator of cardiovascular response and exercise intensity context.

RPE

Subjective indicator of perceived exercise effort.

VO₂

Reflects oxygen consumption and aerobic metabolic demand.

VCO₂

Provides information about carbon dioxide production.

RER

Useful for interpreting whole-body gas exchange and substrate oxidation context.

Lactate

Can help characterise metabolic response when measured appropriately.

RER = VCO₂ / VO₂

Mini Calculation

During exercise, VO₂ = 2.0 L/min and VCO₂ = 1.8 L/min. Calculate RER.

Important interpretation

RER is not simply “the percentage of carbohydrate burned.” Interpretation depends on exercise conditions, steady state and other physiological factors.

12-week exercise programme

A sedentary adult begins supervised exercise 3 times/week for 12 weeks.

What should a clinical exercise scientist monitor over time?

Monitor adherence, exercise tolerance, HR/RPE responses, functional capacity, relevant clinical variables and outcomes appropriate to the programme. Adaptation should be evaluated against baseline and the exercise dose delivered.
PART 9

Clinical Exercise Scientist Application

Case 1 — Sedentary adult with obesity

A client has low exercise tolerance and becomes breathless early during walking.

Design your thinking, not the final prescription:

  1. What information should you obtain first?
  2. Which intensity indicators could you use?
  3. Which physiological responses should be monitored?
  4. How would you progress the exercise?
  5. What safety/clinical factors need consideration?
A sound approach starts with assessment and clinical context, then selects an appropriate mode, initial intensity, duration and monitoring strategy. Progression should be individualised and based on tolerance and response rather than a one-size-fits-all rule.

Case 2 — High-intensity intervals

A participant completes repeated 30-second high-intensity cycling intervals.

Explain why rapid ATP regeneration is important and why recovery periods also involve oxidative metabolism.

The work interval creates a rapid ATP demand. Fast ATP-regeneration pathways contribute strongly. During recovery, oxidative metabolism contributes to restoration of energy stores and physiological recovery. The pathways overlap rather than operating as isolated switches.

Case 3 — Prolonged endurance exercise

A trained cyclist performs 2 hours of moderate exercise.

Identify at least five variables that could influence the metabolic response.

Exercise intensity, duration, training status, glycogen availability, nutritional state, pacing, environment, hydration and individual physiological characteristics can influence the response.
