1. The ATP-PCr System (Phosphagen System)

Simple Explanation

Think of the ATP-PCr system as a "battery jump-starter". When you sprint, the tiny amount of ATP stored in your muscle runs out in 1-2 seconds. Phosphocreatine (PCr) is sitting nearby. It quickly donates a piece of itself (a phosphate) to rebuild ATP instantly. It's incredibly fast, but only lasts about 10 seconds.

Scientific Detail

Where: Cytoplasm (Sarcoplasm) of the muscle cell.

Why: To match massive, immediate energy turnover demands.

Mechanism: The enzyme Creatine Kinase facilitates the breakdown of PCr into Creatine (Cr) and an inorganic phosphate (Pi). The energy released couples with ADP to resynthesize ATP.

PCr + ADP + H+ ⇌ ATP + Cr

Notice how the Pi breaks from Creatine (Cr) to bond with ADP, creating ATP.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"Creatine supplements give you magical energy."

Reality: Creatine supplementation simply increases the intramuscular store of PCr by about 20%. This doesn't make you faster, but it allows the ATP-PCr system to last a few seconds longer before fatiguing, and speeds up recovery between short, explosive bouts (like weightlifting sets).

LECTURER NOTE: Run the animation. Emphasize the reversible arrow in the equation. Ask the class: "If this system is exhausted in 10 seconds, what happens when we run a 400m sprint (approx 50 seconds)?" Lead into Glycolysis.

2. Glycolysis & Lactate Metabolism

When exercise lasts longer than 10 seconds (e.g., 400m sprint), the ATP-PCr system depletes. The body must rapidly break down carbohydrates to sustain ATP regeneration.

THINK FIRST

Where do the carbohydrates come from during high-intensity exercise?

The Fate of Pyruvate

Glycolysis breaks a 6-carbon glucose molecule into two 3-carbon Pyruvate molecules. Pyruvate sits at a metabolic crossroads depending on exercise intensity.

Moderate Intensity (Aerobic)

Energy demand is manageable. Oxygen availability is adequate.

  • Pyruvate enters mitochondria.
  • Converts to Acetyl-CoA.
  • Massive ATP yield (slower).

High Intensity (Anaerobic)

Energy demand is massive. Mitochondria cannot process pyruvate fast enough.

  • Pyruvate accumulates in cytosol.
  • Converted to Lactate by LDH.
  • Fast ATP yield, but causes fatigue.

The Rescue Mechanism

Glycolysis requires a continuous supply of NAD+ to keep running. During intense exercise, NAD+ is quickly consumed and turns into NADH. If NAD+ runs out, glycolysis stops, and the muscle fails.

By converting Pyruvate to Lactate, the enzyme Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) takes the hydrogen off NADH, regenerating NAD+. Therefore, lactate formation allows glycolysis to continue during a sprint. It is a rescue mechanism, not a waste product!

LECTURER NOTE: This is a major paradigm shift for students. Emphasize strongly that lactate does NOT cause delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS). Lactate clears from the blood within 60 minutes post-exercise.

3. Aerobic Metabolism: Fat Oxidation

For long-duration, low-to-moderate intensity exercise, fat is the primary metabolic engine. It yields massive ATP but requires a complex, highly oxygen-dependent process.

1. Lipolysis

Adipose/Muscle

Triglycerides are broken down into 1 Glycerol and 3 Free Fatty Acids (FFAs) by Hormone Sensitive Lipase (HSL).

2. Beta-Oxidation

Mitochondria

A spiral pathway chops the long FFA carbon chains into 2-carbon molecules called Acetyl-CoA. Produces NADH and FADH2.

3. TCA & ETC

Mitochondria

Acetyl-CoA enters the Krebs Cycle. Electrons are passed down the Electron Transport Chain to Oxygen, producing massive ATP.

Calculation Activity: ATP Yield

Let's compare the energy density of a single glucose molecule (6 carbons) to a typical Palmitic acid molecule (16-carbon fatty acid).

Glucose (CHO)

Net Glycolysis: 2 ATP

Krebs Cycle: 2 ATP

ETC (from NADH/FADH2): ~28-32 ATP

Total: ~32-36 ATP

Palmitic Acid (Fat)

Activation: -2 ATP (Cost)

7 rounds Beta-Oxidation: 28 ATP

8 Acetyl-CoA in Krebs: 80 ATP

Total: 106 ATP

Fat is highly dense but metabolically slow.

LECTURER NOTE: Emphasize that while fat yields ~106 ATP, it requires significantly more Oxygen per ATP generated compared to carbohydrates. This is why we shift to carbs as intensity (and oxygen demand) increases.

4. Protein Metabolism & The Alanine Cycle

Under normal exercise conditions, protein contributes less than 5% to energy turnover. However, during starvation, ultra-endurance events, or severe glycogen depletion, amino acids become critical.

The Glucose-Alanine Cycle

When muscle glycogen is low, muscle proteins breakdown into amino acids. The amino group is transferred to pyruvate (from glycolysis) to form Alanine.

Muscle
Pyruvate + NH2 → Alanine
Bloodstream
Liver
Alanine → Pyruvate → Glucose
(Gluconeogenesis)

The newly formed glucose is sent back through the blood to the muscle to fuel exercise.

5. System Integration: The Crossover Concept

The body never uses just one energy system. There is a continuous dynamic shift based on Intensity and Duration.

6. Clinical Application: Case Studies

Apply your knowledge of metabolic pathways to real-world athlete scenarios.

Case 1: "The Heavy Legs"

PROFILE: 22yo Male, 400m sprinter.

At the 300m mark of his race (approx 38 seconds in), the athlete feels an intense burning sensation in his legs. His stride length shortens, and he rapidly decelerates.

1. Which metabolic system is primarily involved at this exact moment?

Anaerobic Glycolysis.

2. Physiologically, why is he decelerating?

The rate of ATP demand exceeds aerobic capacity. Pyruvate is converted to lactate, generating H+ ions. The drop in muscle pH (acidity) inhibits glycolytic enzymes (like PFK) and interferes with calcium binding, impairing muscle contraction.

Case 2: "Hitting the Wall"

PROFILE: 30yo Female, Marathon runner.

At mile 20 (approx 2 hours, 45 minutes), she suddenly experiences profound fatigue, dizziness, and is forced to drop her pace to a slow walk, despite her breathing and heart rate being manageable.

1. What has likely depleted in her body?

Muscle and Liver Glycogen.

2. Why can't she just use her abundant fat stores to maintain her running pace?

While fat is abundant, Beta-oxidation is a slow process. It cannot synthesize ATP rapidly enough to match the high energy turnover required for a marathon pace. "Fats burn in a carbohydrate flame" - intermediate substrates from glucose are needed to keep the Krebs cycle running efficiently.

7. Summary & Final Synthesis

Key Takeaways

  • Energy Turnover dictates substrate use. High demand = CHO. Low demand = Fat.
  • All three energy systems operate continuously; their relative contribution changes based on intensity and duration.
  • Lactate is a rescue mechanism to regenerate NAD+ and allow anaerobic glycolysis to continue, not a metabolic waste product.
  • Training adaptations (more mitochondria, capillarization) shift the crossover point, allowing athletes to spare glycogen and burn fat at higher intensities.
