Foundation 1

FOOD → SUBSTRATES

Simple explanation

Your body does not pour “energy” directly from food into the muscle. Food is digested into smaller molecules. Those molecules become substrates that can be stored, transported and metabolised to regenerate ATP.

FOOD → DIGESTION → ABSORPTION → CIRCULATING SUBSTRATES → CELLULAR UPTAKE

🍚 Carbohydrate

Digestion: starch and disaccharides are broken into monosaccharides, especially glucose.

Storage: glycogen in liver and skeletal muscle.

Exercise relevance: rapid ATP provision; especially important as intensity rises.

Cellular entry: glucose enters muscle through GLUT4. During exercise, muscle contraction promotes GLUT4 translocation independently of insulin. Glycogen can enter glycolysis after mobilisation.

🥑 Fat

Digestion: lipids are emulsified and hydrolysed into fatty acids and monoacylglycerols.

Storage: triglycerides in adipose tissue and intramuscular triglycerides.

Exercise relevance: high energy capacity but slower mobilisation/oxidation than carbohydrate.

Fatty acids enter mitochondria and undergo β-oxidation, producing acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH₂ for oxidative ATP production.

🥩 Protein

Digestion: proteins are broken into amino acids.

Primary role: tissue structure, enzymes, transport and signalling — not a major preferred exercise fuel.

Exercise relevance: contribution can rise during prolonged exercise or glycogen depletion.

Amino acids may be deaminated. Their carbon skeletons can enter pathways linked to TCA intermediates or gluconeogenesis; nitrogen must be handled separately.

KEY FACT

Foundation 2

ENERGY = ATP REQUIREMENT

What is ATP?

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the immediate chemical energy currency used by cells. Muscle contraction consumes ATP. Because stored ATP is small, the body must continuously regenerate ATP from ADP + Pi.

Rate

How quickly can a pathway regenerate ATP?

Capacity

How much total ATP can the pathway help provide?

Power vs capacity

A pathway can be extremely fast without having large total capacity.

Foundation 3

FROM DIGESTION TO THE CELL

1. Digestion

Food is mechanically and chemically processed into smaller molecules.

  • CHO → monosaccharides
  • Fat → fatty acids + monoacylglycerols
  • Protein → amino acids

2. Absorption

Small molecules cross the intestinal epithelium and enter blood or lymph before reaching tissues.

3. Circulation

Substrate availability is influenced by recent food intake, liver metabolism, adipose tissue lipolysis, exercise intensity and hormonal state.

4. Cellular uptake

Muscle cells take up substrates. Glucose can enter through GLUT4; fatty acids are transported and directed toward mitochondrial oxidation.

Important: “Fuel selection” is not simply “carbohydrate OR fat”. Both can be oxidised simultaneously. The relative contribution changes with intensity, duration, training status and substrate availability.

Metabolic pathway 1

ATP-PCr / PHOSPHAGEN SYSTEM

Simple explanation

When ATP demand rises suddenly, the muscle needs an immediate solution. Stored ATP is used first, while phosphocreatine (PCr) donates a phosphate to ADP to rapidly regenerate ATP.

PCr + ADP + H⁺ ⇌ Creatine + ATP

Metabolic pathway 2

GLYCOLYSIS

What is glycolysis?

Glycolysis is a series of reactions in the cytosol that breaks one glucose molecule into two pyruvate molecules. It can supply ATP rapidly without requiring the mitochondria to perform the entire oxidative pathway.

GLUCOSE → 2 PYRUVATE + NET ATP + NADH

Where?

Cytosol of the muscle cell.

Why?

To rapidly transfer chemical energy from carbohydrate into ATP and reduced electron carriers.

When?

Especially important when ATP demand is high and rapid carbohydrate breakdown is required.

Key regulator

Phosphofructokinase (PFK) is a major control point.

FeatureGlycolysis
FuelGlucose or muscle glycogen
LocationCytosol
Key reactions10-step pathway; PFK is a major regulatory step
RateRapid
CapacityGreater than ATP-PCr but limited by substrate, accumulation of metabolites and ability to sustain high glycolytic flux
IntensityModerate-to-high and especially high-intensity work
DurationImportant during efforts lasting tens of seconds to several minutes and during repeated high-intensity bouts
Limiting factorsGlycogen availability, metabolic regulation, H⁺/metabolite disturbance and the ability to regenerate NAD+
By-productsPyruvate, NADH; lactate formation can accompany high glycolytic flux
RecoveryLactate is oxidised or transported; glycogen restoration occurs over a longer time scale

Common misconception: “Lactate is simply a waste product.”
Better: lactate is a metabolically useful intermediate that can be transported and oxidised or converted through pathways such as the Cori cycle.

Metabolic branch point

PYRUVATE → LACTATE OR ACETYL-CoA

Pyruvate is a decision point

After glycolysis, pyruvate can be handled differently depending on metabolic conditions. It can be reduced to lactate to regenerate NAD⁺ rapidly, or enter mitochondrial oxidative metabolism through conversion to acetyl-CoA.

Path A — Lactate

Reaction: pyruvate + NADH ↔ lactate + NAD⁺

Why? Rapid regeneration of NAD⁺ allows glycolysis to continue.

Where? Cytosol.

What next? Lactate can move through blood and tissues, be oxidised, or contribute to glucose production in the liver.

Path B — Acetyl-CoA

Process: pyruvate dehydrogenase complex converts pyruvate to acetyl-CoA.

Where? Mitochondrial matrix.

What next? Acetyl-CoA enters the TCA cycle and supports oxidative phosphorylation.

Cori Cycle — the recovery story

Metabolic pathway 3

KREBS / TCA CYCLE

Simple explanation

The TCA cycle is a mitochondrial pathway that processes acetyl-CoA. Its major value for exercise physiology is not a large direct ATP output. It produces NADH and FADH₂, which carry high-energy electrons to the electron transport chain.

Acetyl-CoA → TCA cycle → NADH + FADH₂ → ETC → ATP
What happens?

Acetyl groups are oxidised through a cyclic series of reactions.

Where?

Mitochondrial matrix.

Why important?

Generates electron carriers that drive high-capacity ATP production.

LECTURER PROMPT: “If the TCA cycle does not make most ATP directly, why is it still essential?”
KEY FACT

Metabolic pathway 4

ELECTRON TRANSPORT CHAIN → OXIDATIVE PHOSPHORYLATION

The big idea

NADH and FADH₂ deliver electrons to the respiratory chain. Electron flow powers proton pumping across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The resulting proton gradient drives ATP synthase to make ATP. Oxygen serves as the final electron acceptor and water is formed.

FeatureOxidative phosphorylation
Fuel inputsNADH/FADH₂ generated from carbohydrate, fat and amino-acid metabolism

By-productsCO₂ and H₂O; heat is released as part of energy transfer

Alternative substrates

FAT OXIDATION + PROTEIN / NITROGEN

Fat → β-oxidation → acetyl-CoA

What: fatty acids are shortened in repeated cycles.

Where: mitochondrial matrix after entry into mitochondria.

Outputs: acetyl-CoA, NADH, FADH₂.

Why: huge energy capacity, but slower ATP supply.

Exercise: relative fat oxidation generally increases at lower/moderate intensities and with endurance training.

Protein → amino acids → carbon skeleton + nitrogen

What: amino acids can be deaminated and their carbon skeletons can enter energy pathways.

Nitrogen: must be handled separately and ultimately excreted through nitrogen disposal pathways.

Exercise: usually a smaller energy contribution than CHO and fat, but may rise during prolonged exercise or low glycogen availability.

Exercise response

ACUTE RESPONSE: WHAT CHANGES DURING EXERCISE?

Cardiovascular

HR and cardiac output rise to support oxygen and nutrient delivery.

Respiratory

Ventilation rises as oxygen demand and CO₂ production increase.

Metabolic

ATP turnover accelerates; substrate mobilisation and pathway flux increase.

Muscle

Motor-unit recruitment and ATP demand rise with external workload.

Thermoregulation

Heat production increases; sweating and skin blood flow contribute to heat dissipation.

Blood chemistry

At higher intensities, lactate and acid-base disturbances become more prominent.

CONNECT THE CHAIN: Exercise demand ↑ → ATP turnover ↑ → substrate use ↑ → O₂/CO₂/HR/VE responses ↑ → recovery begins when demand falls.

Immediate response

IMMEDIATE RECOVERY

Recovery is not “nothing happening”. The body is actively restoring homeostasis: PCr resynthesis, lactate transport/oxidation, oxygen restoration, temperature regulation, fluid balance and glycogen restoration occur on different time scales.

TimeWhat happensWhy it matters
Seconds–minutesHR and ventilation begin falling; PCr resynthesis progresses.Rapid restoration of high-energy phosphate availability.
Minutes–hoursLactate is oxidised or converted; temperature and cardiovascular variables continue toward baseline.Restores metabolic and physiological balance.
Hours+Glycogen restoration and tissue recovery continue depending on nutrition and training load.Determines readiness for subsequent exercise.

Prediction

After a hard interval, which variable should remain elevated for a while: HR, VO₂, or both?

Both. Recovery is dynamic; oxygen consumption may remain elevated above resting levels for a period (EPOC), while HR also declines progressively.

Real-life application • guided reasoning

🕵️ METABOLISM DETECTIVE LAB

Use one repeatable reasoning chain: DEMAND → FUEL → PATHWAY → ATP RATE → MEASUREMENT → INTERPRETATION → RECOVERY.

How to use: Predict first. Identify the substrate and pathway. Then choose the variable that would provide evidence. Reveal the mechanism only after you have reasoned through the case.
CASE 01 • FASTING

Why can fasting change breath odour?

With prolonged fasting, carbohydrate availability falls and fat mobilisation increases.

Mechanism: fatty acids → β-oxidation → acetyl-CoA → liver ketogenesis → ketone bodies. Acetone is volatile and can be exhaled.

What changed in substrate availability?

With prolonged fasting, liver glycogen becomes limited and fatty-acid oxidation increases. The liver produces ketone bodies; acetone can contribute to fruity/acetone-like breath odour. Safety: fruity breath with vomiting, abdominal pain, deep/rapid breathing or marked hyperglycaemia can indicate diabetic ketoacidosis and requires urgent medical attention.
CASE 02 • CARBOHYDRATE LOADING

Why carbohydrate-load before a marathon?

Follow carbohydrate from food to stored fuel.

Mechanism: carbohydrate → glucose → muscle/liver glycogen → greater carbohydrate availability during prolonged exercise.

Does carbohydrate loading create ATP directly?

No. It increases stored carbohydrate availability before prolonged exercise. During exercise, glycogen supplies glucose for glycolysis and subsequent oxidative ATP production, helping delay the point at which carbohydrate availability becomes limiting.
CASE 03 • SPRINT

Why does the first few seconds feel explosive?

ATP demand rises almost immediately, before oxidative metabolism can fully increase.

Mechanism: stored ATP + phosphocreatine → rapid ATP resynthesis.

Which pathway has the fastest ATP regeneration rate?

The ATP-PCr system can regenerate ATP very rapidly and contributes strongly during very short, high-power efforts. It does not work alone; glycolytic and oxidative pathways also contribute as exercise continues.
CASE 04 • 400 m

Why does lactate rise?

ATP demand is very high and glycolytic flux increases substantially.

Mechanism: glycogen/glucose → glycolysis → pyruvate → lactate formation when lactate appearance exceeds immediate utilisation/clearance.

Is lactate simply “waste”?

No. Lactate is a metabolically useful intermediate that can be transported and oxidised or used in other pathways. Blood lactate reflects the balance between lactate appearance and removal, not simply a fatigue toxin.
CASE 05 • 5 KM

Why does VO₂ matter?

A sustained running effort requires substantial aerobic ATP production.

Mechanism: O₂ consumption reflects the rate of aerobic oxidative metabolism under appropriate conditions.

What other variable helps explain the metabolic picture?

VO₂ can be interpreted alongside workload, HR, VCO₂, RER, ventilation and RPE. Together these variables help explain the relationship between external demand and internal physiological response.
CASE 06 • THE “WALL”

Why might a marathoner suddenly slow down?

The athlete still has stored fat, but ATP supply rate may become limiting at the required intensity.

Mechanism: glycogen availability ↓ → greater relative reliance on fat oxidation → ATP supply rate may not support the same high exercise intensity.

Is “having energy” the same as “producing ATP fast enough”?

No. This is the distinction between energy capacity and rate of ATP production. Fat stores have high energy capacity, while carbohydrate pathways can support a higher rate of ATP supply during intense exercise.
CASE 07 • RECOVERY

Why is VO₂ still elevated after exercise?

Exercise has stopped, but recovery processes continue.

Mechanism: PCr restoration + thermoregulation + cardiovascular recovery + metabolic processes contribute to elevated post-exercise oxygen consumption.

What could you monitor?

Monitor HR recovery, VO₂ recovery when available, RPE and other relevant physiological variables. The pattern provides information about how the body returns toward baseline.
CASE 08 • SAME SPEED, DIFFERENT RESPONSE

Why can two people walking at the same speed have different HR and RPE?

The external demand is the same, but the internal physiological cost may differ.

Mechanism: fitness, cardiovascular function, metabolic efficiency, training status, fatigue and health status can alter internal response.

What is the key distinction?

External workload ≠ internal physiological load. This becomes increasingly important when students later learn exercise assessment and prescription.

A person increases treadmill speed. HR, VO₂, VCO₂ and RPE all rise. What changed first: the measurement or the exercise demand?

The external exercise demand changed first. The physiological variables are the body's internal responses to that demand. Core logic: change the demand → observe the response → explain the mechanism.
Case-based learning

APPLY THE WHOLE CHAIN

Case 1 — 400-m runner

Age: 20 • Event: 400 m • Demand: very high intensity

Predict the relative contribution of ATP-PCr, glycolytic and oxidative pathways. Then explain why lactate may rise.

All three contribute. ATP-PCr supports the rapid start; glycolytic contribution becomes substantial as the effort continues; oxidative contribution is present and rises over time. High glycolytic flux increases pyruvate production and lactate formation can accompany rapid NAD⁺ regeneration.

Case 2 — 5-km runner

Age: 24 • Event: 5 km • Demand: sustained high aerobic demand

What changes in substrate contribution and oxygen consumption as pace increases?

Oxidative metabolism provides a large share of ATP, while glycolytic and phosphagen pathways still contribute. As pace/intensity rises, carbohydrate contribution generally increases and VO₂ rises toward the athlete's upper capacity.

Case 3 — 2-hour ride

Scenario: moderate endurance cycling with limited carbohydrate intake.

Predict what happens to substrate contribution as duration progresses.

Oxidative metabolism dominates. Fat oxidation can contribute substantially, while carbohydrate remains important. With prolonged duration and declining glycogen availability, maintaining high intensity becomes more difficult.

Case 4 — interval training

Scenario: 10 × 30 s hard efforts with 90 s recovery.

Why does recovery quality affect the next interval?

Recovery allows partial PCr resynthesis, lactate/metabolite handling and cardiovascular/respiratory recovery. Incomplete recovery changes the starting state of the next interval, so the same external workload may produce a different internal response.
