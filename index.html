02 • FROM FOOD TO FUEL

First: Where does exercise energy come from?

Your muscle does not use a plate of rice, a banana or a piece of chicken directly to make a contraction. Food must first be digested, absorbed, transported and metabolically processed.

The journey from meal to muscle

🍽️ Food→Digestion→Absorption→Blood→Muscle cell→Metabolism→ATP

The three major macronutrients contribute differently. Carbohydrate is closely linked to glucose and glycogen metabolism; fat provides fatty acids; protein provides amino acids whose carbon skeletons can enter metabolic pathways.

CARBOHYDRATE

🍚 What happens to carbohydrate?

Dietary carbohydrate is digested into smaller sugars. Glucose enters the circulation and can be stored as glycogen in liver and skeletal muscle or used in energy metabolism.

Exercise connection: Muscle glycogen is an important carbohydrate store for exercise. During exercise, glycogen can provide glucose for glycolysis. Carbohydrate becomes particularly important as exercise intensity increases because it can support rapid ATP production.
FAT

🥑 What happens to fat?

Dietary fat is processed largely as triglycerides and fatty acids. Stored body fat provides a very large energy reserve.

Exercise connection: Fatty acids can be transported to mitochondria and undergo β-oxidation. Fat oxidation is important during rest and lower-to-moderate intensity/prolonged exercise, although substrate use changes with intensity and nutritional state.
PROTEIN

🍗 What happens to protein?

Protein is digested into amino acids. Amino acids primarily support tissue synthesis and other functions, but some can contribute to energy metabolism.

Exercise connection: Amino acid metabolism separates nitrogen handling from the carbon skeleton. The carbon skeleton can enter metabolic pathways; nitrogen is handled through pathways including urea formation. Protein is generally a smaller direct energy contributor than carbohydrate and fat during most exercise conditions.
03 • ATP: THE IMMEDIATE ENERGY CURRENCY

What exactly is ATP?

Before learning the energy systems, students need one idea: muscle work requires ATP, and ATP must be continuously regenerated.

ATP = adenosine triphosphate

ATP contains adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups. The molecule can participate in energy-coupling reactions that support cellular work.

⚡
ATP→ADP + Pi+usable energy

Why can't we simply store lots of ATP?

The amount of ATP stored in skeletal muscle is relatively small. If exercise demand suddenly rises, the body therefore needs systems that can regenerate ATP at different speeds.

Small ATP store+Huge exercise demand→continuous ATP regeneration
Key question: not “Which energy system is on?” but “Which pathways are contributing most strongly to meet the current ATP demand?” All three major systems can contribute simultaneously.

ATP regeneration: three routes

FASTEST

ATP-PCr

Uses stored ATP and phosphocreatine to regenerate ATP rapidly.

FAST

Glycolysis

Breaks down glucose/glycogen through a series of reactions to pyruvate, with rapid ATP generation.

SUSTAINED

Oxidative metabolism

Uses mitochondrial pathways, oxygen-dependent electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation to support prolonged ATP supply.

04 • GLYCOLYSIS

What is glycolysis?

Glycolysis is a series of reactions that breaks one glucose molecule into two pyruvate molecules. It occurs in the cytosol and does not directly require oxygen.

The big picture

Glucose (6C)→Glycolysis→2 Pyruvate (3C each)

1. Investment

Early reactions use ATP to prepare and rearrange glucose. Think of this as an energy investment required to make the pathway proceed.

2. Splitting

The six-carbon molecule is rearranged and eventually split into two three-carbon molecules.

3. Payoff

Later reactions generate ATP and NADH. Because two three-carbon molecules continue through the pathway, the products are doubled.

What does glycolysis produce?

  • 2 pyruvate per glucose
  • Net 2 ATP per glucose through substrate-level phosphorylation
  • NADH, which can support further ATP production under oxidative conditions

Important: exact ATP accounting can depend on the substrate and shuttle assumptions used in the teaching model. For this course, focus on the pathway logic and exercise application.

What happens to pyruvate?

Pyruvate→lactate
Pyruvate→acetyl-CoA

The metabolic fate depends on exercise intensity, glycolytic flux, mitochondrial oxidative capacity and cellular conditions. Lactate is not simply a “waste product”; it can be transported and metabolised.

Exercise example

A student performs repeated high-intensity intervals. ATP demand rises sharply. Glycolytic flux increases, producing pyruvate rapidly. Some pyruvate is converted to lactate. At the same time, oxidative metabolism continues.

HR, RPE, workload/power, VO₂, VCO₂ and—when appropriate—blood lactate can help describe the exercise response. No single variable gives the entire metabolic picture.
05 • PYRUVATE & LACTATE

Lactate: not simply “waste”

Students often learn “lack of oxygen → lactic acid.” That explanation is too simplistic for exercise physiology.

What actually matters?

During high glycolytic flux, pyruvate can be reduced to lactate while NAD⁺ is regenerated, allowing glycolysis to continue. Lactate can then be transported and used by other tissues or converted back through metabolic pathways.

Pyruvate↔Lactate

Why does blood lactate rise?

Blood lactate concentration reflects the balance between production, transport and clearance/utilisation. It is therefore influenced by exercise intensity, duration, muscle recruitment and recovery.

When exercise stops, lactate concentration can fall as lactate is oxidised or otherwise metabolised. Active recovery may alter the rate of decline compared with complete rest, depending on conditions.

07 • KREBS / TCA CYCLE

What is the Krebs cycle?

The citric acid cycle (TCA cycle or Krebs cycle) is a series of mitochondrial reactions that oxidise acetyl-CoA and generate reducing equivalents that can support ATP production through the electron transport chain.

Where does it fit?

Glucose→Pyruvate→Acetyl-CoA→TCA→NADH/FADH₂→ETC→ATP

The cycle idea

Acetyl-CoA enters a cyclic series of reactions. Carbon atoms are progressively oxidised, and the cycle captures energy mainly in the form of NADH and FADH₂, plus a small amount of GTP/ATP depending on the accounting system.

The key exercise concept is not memorising every intermediate. It is understanding that the TCA cycle is a central hub connecting carbohydrate, fat and some amino acid metabolism to oxidative ATP production.

Why is it important in exercise?

  • Supports sustained aerobic ATP production.
  • Receives acetyl-CoA derived from carbohydrate and fatty acid metabolism.
  • Produces NADH/FADH₂ that deliver high-energy electrons to the ETC.
  • Its capacity and regulation are relevant to endurance training adaptation.

08 • ELECTRON TRANSPORT CHAIN

Where is most oxidative ATP production supported?

The electron transport chain is located in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electrons from NADH and FADH₂ support proton movement, creating a gradient that powers ATP synthase.

NADH / FADH₂→Electron transport→H⁺ gradient→ATP synthase→ATP

1. Electron flow

Electrons move through protein complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

2. Proton gradient

Energy from electron transfer supports proton pumping, creating an electrochemical gradient.

3. ATP synthase

Proton movement through ATP synthase drives phosphorylation of ADP to ATP.

Oxygen's role: Oxygen acts as the terminal electron acceptor in the electron transport chain, ultimately allowing oxidative phosphorylation to continue.
09 • FAT OXIDATION

How does fat become ATP?

Fat provides a very large energy store, but accessing and oxidising fatty acids involves more steps than using stored ATP or rapidly mobilised carbohydrate.

Triglyceride→Fatty acid→β-oxidation→Acetyl-CoA→TCA→ETC→ATP

Why is fat useful?

It stores a large amount of chemical energy and becomes an important oxidative substrate during rest and many prolonged, lower-to-moderate intensity exercise conditions.

Why can't fat simply replace carbohydrate at every intensity?

Fat oxidation involves mobilisation, transport and mitochondrial processing. Carbohydrate can support higher rates of ATP production and is therefore increasingly important as exercise intensity rises.

10 • PROTEIN & NITROGEN

What happens to amino acids?

Protein metabolism requires students to distinguish the nitrogen component from the carbon skeleton.

Protein→Amino acid→Carbon skeleton+Nitrogen

Carbon skeleton

Can enter metabolic pathways at different points and contribute to energy metabolism.

Nitrogen

Requires handling because nitrogen-containing compounds must be metabolised and excreted appropriately.

Urea / alanine relationship

Exercise physiology can use the glucose-alanine cycle to illustrate transport of carbon and nitrogen between muscle and liver.

11 • ENERGY SYSTEM INTEGRATION

All three systems work together

The classic mistake is to say “10 seconds = ATP-PCr, 30 seconds = glycolysis, 2 minutes = aerobic.” Real exercise is more integrated.

ATP-PCr

Very rapid

Stored ATP and phosphocreatine provide rapid ATP regeneration. Particularly important when ATP demand rises suddenly.

Example: maximal sprint, jump, heavy lift.
GLYCOLYTIC

Rapid carbohydrate breakdown

Glycolysis can increase ATP production rapidly. It becomes particularly important during high-intensity exercise when carbohydrate demand is high.

Example: hard interval, repeated sprint.
OXIDATIVE

Sustained ATP production

Mitochondrial oxidative metabolism supports prolonged ATP production and contributes during shorter exercise too.

Example: distance running, cycling, recovery.

15 • ACUTE RESPONSE → RECOVERY → ADAPTATION

What changes immediately, and what changes after training?

ACUTE

During one exercise bout

HR, ventilation, VO₂, energy demand and substrate use change rapidly in response to the exercise stimulus.

RECOVERY

Minutes to hours

ATP-PCr resynthesis, lactate handling, restoration of fluid and substrate stores and other recovery processes occur.

CHRONIC

Repeated training

Repeated exercise can produce adaptations such as improved oxidative capacity, metabolic efficiency and ability to sustain a given workload.

16 • CASE-BASED LEARNING

Now apply the whole story

Use: food → substrate → pathway → ATP → exercise demand → monitoring → recovery.

CASE 1

10-second maximal sprint

Which ATP-regenerating pathway is particularly important?

Correct. ATP-PCr provides very rapid ATP regeneration, while other pathways also contribute.
CASE 2

Hard interval

HR, RPE and lactate rise. What is the best interpretation?

Correct. High-intensity exercise increases ATP demand and glycolytic contribution; oxidative metabolism still contributes.
CASE 3

30-minute cycling

Which system becomes particularly important for sustained ATP supply?

Correct. Sustained exercise depends substantially on oxidative metabolism using carbohydrate and fat according to conditions.
CASE 4

2-hour recovery

The athlete has stopped exercising. Which statement is most appropriate?

Correct. Recovery is metabolically active. Substrate restoration, fluid balance and tissue recovery can continue.
